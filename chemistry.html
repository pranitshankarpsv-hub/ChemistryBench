Everything starts with the atom

One number decides what an element is — add or remove a single proton and you have a different substance with different chemistry, which is why the table is ordered by that count and nothing else.

The colours mark families, and families exist because atoms with a nearly-filled outer shell behave alike; the neutron count is deliberately loose, and that freedom is exactly what isotopes are.

Every element, drawn as far as we can honestly draw it

An atom is mostly empty space wrapped around a nucleus dense enough to define the element, and electrons fill the lowest-energy shells first — which is why a 4s electron turns up before a 3d one.

Protons and electrons are drawn in equal numbers so neutrality is something you see rather than something you are told; this is a Bohr teaching model, and real orbitals are probability clouds with no edge to outline.

Different nuclei from the same ingredients

Protons decide which element you are, so changing only the neutron count gives isotopes of one element — including the radioactive ones nobody can find in the ground and has to make.

Hold the neutron count fixed and change the protons instead, and you get isobars: the same total mass spread across different elements, which is exactly where stability stops being predictable.

Watch matter change state

Push past a transition point and the reading freezes, because the energy you keep adding goes into tearing structure apart rather than into raising the temperature.

That plateau is the entire experiment, and these are real melting and boiling points at 1 atm — superheavy values nobody has ever measured say so instead of inventing a number.

Build it from two ions

Pick one positive and one negative ion and the formula stops being a choice and becomes arithmetic: the charges must cancel exactly, which is why aluminium sulfate always needs two Al3+ for every three SO42−.

Everything here is derived from valency rather than memorised — the fewest ions always wins, and the bracket rule exists only so a multi-atom group is never misread as a single atom. Not sure what to try? Open the table.
